Case study 01 · GHCM

Designing systems around how a retail business actually works.

The important work was not the public website. It was a connected set of internal systems serving store managers, accounting staff, inventory users, marketing staff, and customers—each with a different view of the same business.

Period2002–2012
RoleCo-owner · UX lead · Business analyst · Project manager
ContextFive New York City retail stores
FocusFinance · Inventory · Customer communication

Context

Design from inside the business.

GHCM grew from one clothing store to five locations in Brooklyn and Manhattan, with approximately fifteen full-time employees plus part-time staff at its peak. I was not an outside designer learning the operation through a short discovery phase; I was helping run it.

That proximity made the systems less abstract. Financial reporting, inventory control, targeted email, store operations, equipment, and staff workflows were parts of the same operating environment.

Income & expense reporting Inventory management Customer email Store portal / CMS Employee timekeeping External sites

01 · Financial reporting

One set of data. Several ways of understanding it.

Store managers and accounting staff worked with the same financial information, but they organized it differently. The design preserved one source of truth while letting each role begin with the business question appropriate to its work.

Company or store → expense category → individual transaction.

Progressive disclosure supported both scanning and investigation. Functions appeared at the level where they became relevant instead of competing for attention on the first screen.

02 · Inventory

“Easy to use” depended on who was using it.

Inventory users differed sharply in confidence and speed. Rather than force both groups through one compromised interface, the system offered two paths built around different kinds of simplicity.

Expert user

Visibility and speed

  • More information available at once
  • Fast repetitive entry
  • Fewer interruptions and extra steps
  • Efficiency over guided simplicity
More deliberate user

Guidance and error prevention

  • Fewer simultaneous choices
  • Reduced cognitive load
  • More deliberate progression
  • Lower opportunity for input error

03 · Customer communication

Customer behavior became part of the next interaction.

The email system did more than send a message. It let staff build audiences from buying characteristics—including designer, style, size, season, and occasion—and then independently select content and visual treatment.

Select audience→Select content→Select visual treatment→Preview→Send or schedule

Separating those decisions allowed the audience, message, and presentation to vary independently while remaining part of one understandable workflow.

This is early evidence of a theme that appears much later in my career: customer information becomes valuable when it changes what the organization does next.

04 · External sites

The visually obvious work was not the important work.

The public sites establish breadth and implementation experience. But they were relatively simple portals. The stronger experience-design story was in the operational systems behind the business.

What deserved emphasis instead

Role-based financial information, expert and guided inventory workflows, operational drill-down, accountability, and customer segmentation were the more substantial design problems.

The case is therefore about experience design across the operating system of a retail business—not simply web design for a retailer.

Case takeaway

The interface followed the user’s relationship to the business.

Accounting staff, store managers, expert operators, less-confident users, and marketing staff were not forced into the same representation. Shared systems were shaped around how each group needed to understand and act.

Role-based information architecture Progressive disclosure and complex data Expert versus guided interaction design Customer segmentation and workflow design

This establishes the case-study pattern: context, a small number of design decisions, surviving evidence, honest limitations, and a concise takeaway.
